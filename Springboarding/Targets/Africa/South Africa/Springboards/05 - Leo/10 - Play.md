## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Vatsonga and VhaVenda in Limpopo:** check whether games recorded by Junod and Stayt are still played and under what names.
- **Basotho of the Free State and Bapedi of Limpopo:** check girls' stone-storytelling and riddle sessions as play kept by girls and grandmothers.
- **Herd boys in rural KwaZulu-Natal and the Eastern Cape:** check stick play as children's game against its role in adult contests.
- **Batswana of North West and isiZulu speakers:** compare morabaraba and umlabalaba rules and where tournaments draw players.
- **Afrikaners of the former Transvaal and Free State:** check kennetjie, volkspele and jukskei memories, and the Sunday rules that limited them.
- **Coloured communities of the Cape Flats, in their own self-designation:** check street games, joking duels and comedy as everyday play.
- **Township children in Gauteng and eThekwini:** check street soccer and homemade toys against access to parks and early-learning centers.
- **!Xun and Khwe children at Platfontein:** check play recorded by community projects and schools, in their own languages.
- **Gamers in Gauteng and Western Cape cities:** check who plays video games competitively, by gender and language.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for older ethnographies of games and for children's television; mixed for measured play time and parks; thin for Khoe and San children's games after the nineteenth century and for siSwati and isiNdebele games.
- **Languages needed:** Xitsonga and Tshivenda for Junod's and Stayt's game names; Sesotho for dilotho and masekitlana; Afrikaans for volkspele and Sabbath records; Kaaps for comedy.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** stick contests can involve serious injury; some games belong to initiation and are not for outsiders; colonial ethnographers framed children's games as signs of "primitiveness".
- **Held for review:** children's games, toys and festival play kept by South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands, outside the scope of this play sheet.
