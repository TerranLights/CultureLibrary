# Manchuria: Education (Sagittarius): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Manchuria, region East Asia. **Language(s) for searching:** Chinese, Korean, Mongolian, Manchu, Russian and Japanese (for the railway and occupation eras); Hui Muslim school vocabulary. **Concept in one line:** formal and higher learning (schools, universities, degrees, scholarship and lifelong study) and the standing of the educated person.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Families of state-enterprise workers** versus farm families: whether a degree meant a secure post or a ticket out.
- **Yanbian Koreans:** high esteem for schooling is a claim to test against data on Korean-school enrollment and migration.
- **Mongols** of the eastern banners and their Mongolian-medium schools.
- **Manchu revival learners** versus Manchus with no access to the language.
- **Oroqen, Evenki, Daur and Hezhe** children in boarding schools.
- **Hui** families balancing mosque and state schooling.
- **Harbin and Dalian** (university and port cities) versus forest and border towns with few colleges.
- **Graduates who stay** versus those who leave for Beijing, Shanghai or abroad.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for statistics and institutional histories; mixed for how families judge the worth of schooling.
- **Languages needed:** Chinese for school records and gazetteers; Korean for Yanbian schooling; Mongolian and Manchu for banner and examination sources; Japanese for Manchukuo-era schools; Russian for Harbin's schools.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** changes to minority-language schooling (Korean and Mongolian) are politically sensitive; Cultural Revolution treatment of teachers is guarded; Manchukuo-era schooling carries collaboration questions.
- **Held for review:** none.
