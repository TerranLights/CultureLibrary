# Xiang: Surrender (Pisces): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Xiang 湘, Sinian Federation member state (Hunan 湖南), region East Asia. **Language(s) for searching:** Standard Chinese; classical Chinese for Chan records and Ming loyalist writing; Changsha Xiang (the sayings of giving in and refusing to); the Southwestern Mandarin of the northwest; Tujia and Miao for memories of conquest. **Concept in one line:** ceasing to resist; giving oneself, one's will or one's control over to something stronger or higher (God, fate, a lover, an enemy, illness, death); release and letting go.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Xiang valley towns tied to the Xiang Army story: the strongest "never yield" narrative; test it against ordinary memory.
- Hengyang: Ming loyalism and the 1944 siege, two memories of refusing and yielding.
- Huaihua and Zhijiang: a town whose name is tied to an enemy's surrender.
- Tujia of Yongshun and Longshan; Miao of Fenghuang: conquest and submission as seen from the conquered side.
- Buddhist lay circles: letting go as practice.
- Rural elders and hospital patients in Changsha: acceptance of illness and death.
- Yao of Jianghua and Dong of Tongdao: funerals and release rites of their own.
- Elderly Hunanese living with children in other provinces: comparison only, for acceptance of illness and age away from home.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for war and political surrender; mixed for religious letting-go; thin for everyday giving in (quarrels, quitting).
- **Languages needed:** classical Chinese; Standard Chinese; Changsha Xiang for sayings; Tujia and Miao for conquest memory.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** 1949 and the political campaigns remain sensitive; Ming-Qing conquest touches ethnic memory; end-of-life decisions are private; the "never yield" tag can harden into a stereotype.
- **Held for review:** none.
