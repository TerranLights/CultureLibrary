*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Minangkabau**, for whom leaving is a social duty of young men; test change for women and for today.
- **Bugis, Makassar and Mandar**, whose sailing out built settlements across the archipelago.
- **Bajo** sea people, for mobility without a fixed home port.
- **Banjar**, migrants along the Sumatran and Malaysian coasts.
- **Javanese**, as transmigrants and the largest mudik population, set against their own stay-at-home proverb.
- **Madurese**, migrants to Kalimantan, where conflict with Dayak hosts followed (Sampit, 2001; sensitive).
- **Batak**, urban migrants to Jakarta and Medan; test, don't assume, the trade stereotypes.
- **Balinese**, as both pilgrims (tirtayatra) and hosts to mass tourism.
- **Sasak** of Lombok and people of **East Nusa Tenggara**, among the main senders of migrant workers.
- **Chinese Indonesians** (peranakan and totok), whose arrival stories are migration histories.
- **Arab-Indonesians**, whose ties run to Hadhramaut.
- Diaspora, comparison only: Indonesian plantation workers in Sabah, to see whether the rantau ethic survives across a border.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich (mobility is heavily counted, and merantau and the hajj are well studied); thin for small-island and sea-people travel.
- **Languages needed:** Indonesian; Minangkabau, Buginese and Javanese for regional sources; Old Javanese and Old Sundanese for the classics; Arabic for hajj texts.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** migrant workers' abuse and deaths; trafficking from Nusa Tenggara; transmigration's conflicts with host peoples; Sampit.
- **Held for review:** none.
