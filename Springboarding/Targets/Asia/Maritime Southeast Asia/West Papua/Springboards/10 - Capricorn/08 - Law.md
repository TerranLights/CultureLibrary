# West Papua: Law (Capricorn): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** West Papua, region Maritime Southeast Asia. **Language(s) for searching:** Indonesian and Papuan Malay; Dutch for the first meeting of colonial and customary law; English for comparative legal anthropology; Mee, Asmat, Hubula (Dani) and Arfak languages for each people's own rules. **Concept in one line:** the binding rules of a community, and how people relate to them: what law is for, whose it is, whether it is just, and whether to obey it, use it, bend it or avoid it. This sheet takes the spirit of the law; statutes and courts as written are Libra's.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Central Highlands and Baliem (Mee, Hubula, Lani, Yali):** check where clan forums handle killings and where the police take over, and which side people choose.
- **South coast, Asmat and Kamoro:** check how the colonial ban and the mission reshaped ideas of rightful punishment.
- **Bird's Head (Arfak peoples, Moi, Maybrat):** look at older customary rules and their revival in regency regulations.
- **Jayapura and Sentani:** compare town crowds, courts and the customary council in the capital area.
- **Merauke lowlands (Marind, Kanum, Muyu):** check how custom is invoked in land and family cases before state courts.
- **Biak and Cenderawasih Bay:** look at political trials and how defendants from the coast spoke of law.
- **Raja Ampat and Fakfak:** check how religious law, old raja authority and state law are weighed in Muslim and mixed villages.
- **Migrant settler communities:** settlers' own use of police and courts is part of today's towns; treat it as a separate pattern.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** mixed. Legal anthropology of the Mee is deep; for most peoples, how people relate to law is known only through the press and a few theses.
- **Languages needed:** Indonesian for trials and theses; Dutch for the colonial ban; Mee and Asmat for customary concepts that do not map onto "law".
- **Sensitive or restricted:** treason trials and the Enembe case are political; customary rules may be closed knowledge; never name private parties in crowd-justice cases.
- **Held for review:** none; the legal-culture pointers set aside nothing under the rules.

## 5. First queries
1. Read Pospisil (1971) and note his criteria for what counts as law.
2. Search the Uncen repository for "kesadaran hukum" with the names of six regencies.
3. Collect two or three published *pledoi* from Papuan treason trials.
4. Pull the police-reporting tables from BPS *Statistik Kriminal* for the Papuan provinces.
5. Search the Supreme Court decisions directory for Wamena and Merauke rulings that cite *hukum adat*.
