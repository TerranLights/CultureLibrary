## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **East and West, and Berlin's two halves:** post-1990 self-labels and generational split (born before or after the Wall fell).
- **Regional self-labels:** Rhinelanders, Westphalians, Hessians, Saxons, Thuringians, Mecklenburgers, Hanseatic city people; Baden and Württemberg as rivals inside one state; the Saar, which joined later.
- **The four recognized national minorities (Danes, Frisians, Sorbs, German Sinti and Roma):** identity carried by schools, media and associations.
- **Descendants of expellees from the former eastern territories:** an origin that no longer exists as a German place.
- **Germans from Russia and Kazakhstan:** German on paper, often called Russian in daily life.
- **Turkish- and Kurdish-heritage Germans, Alevis separately:** labels from both sides, by generation.
- **Jewish communities:** long-rooted families against post-1990 arrivals from the former USSR.
- **Afro-Germans, Polish-heritage families (Ruhr miners' descendants), Vietnamese Germans:** different routes to a hyphen.
- **Recent Syrian, Afghan and Ukrainian arrivals:** identity in the first decade.
- **Confession as identity:** Catholic Rhineland and Münsterland, Protestant north, the largely unaffiliated East.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich; surveys, museums and minority bodies abound. Thin for very local identities beyond the big dialect areas.
- **Languages needed:** German; Sorbian, Danish and Frisian for minority bodies; Turkish, Russian and Kurdish for heritage communities.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** "Volk", "Heimat" and "Leitkultur" carry Nazi-era and political baggage; expellee material ranges from scholarship to revanchist writing; read critically.
- **Related signs:** belonging (Cancer), heritage (Capricorn), community (Aquarius).
- **Held for review:** none.
