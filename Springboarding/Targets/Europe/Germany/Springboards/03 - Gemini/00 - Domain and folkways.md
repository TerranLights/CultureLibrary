## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **The Low German north** (Schleswig-Holstein, Hamburg, Bremen, Lower Saxony, Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania): a separate regional language with its own theater, radio and school teaching.
- **The Rhineland and the Ruhr:** urban speech (Kölsch, Ruhr German) shaped by carnival and by mining-era migration.
- **Württemberg and Baden:** Swabian and Alemannic speech, often kept in public use longer than in the north.
- **Saxony, Thuringia and Brandenburg:** school, neighbor and workplace habits formed under the GDR.
- **Sorbs of Upper and Lower Lusatia:** a Slavic-speaking minority with bilingual schools and churches.
- **Danes and North Frisians of Schleswig:** small minorities with their own schools, press and island varieties.
- **German Sinti and Roma:** a recognized minority whose language is kept within the community.
- **Turkish- and Kurdish-German, Russian-German and Arabic-speaking households:** the largest home-language communities after German.
- **The Deaf community:** DGS users, with their own clubs and associations.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for dialects, corpora, schooling and travel; thinner, mostly survey-based, for neighbors and everyday talk.
- **Languages needed:** German first; Low German, Sorbian, Frisian and Danish for minority sources; Turkish, Russian and Arabic for immigrant media.
- **Scope:** Germany's exact post-war lands are still to be settled. These sheets are written for the German lands outside Bavaria, which belongs to the separate Austro-Bavaria target and appears here only as a comparison.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** Nazi-era folklore and dialect work (Volkskunde atlases of the 1930s) needs critical reading; Sinti and Roma sources should be read as the community publishes them; migrants' language is a political topic: separate data from polemic.
- **Held for review:** none.
