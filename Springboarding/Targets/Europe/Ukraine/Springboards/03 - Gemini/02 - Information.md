## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Front-line and border oblasts (Kharkiv, Sumy, Zaporizhzhia, Kherson) and the far west:** compare which channels people rely on for alerts and news in media surveys since 2022.
- **People in occupied areas and those who left them:** check how news crosses the line (VPN, relatives, radio) through displaced people's accounts; direct surveying is not possible.
- **Russian-speaking and Ukrainian-speaking audiences in Odesa and Dnipro:** check trust in each channel and language, without assuming either.
- **Crimean Tatars:** check which outlets and messenger groups carry community news, and how exile changed it.
- **Transcarpathian Hungarians and Bukovinian Romanians:** compare reliance on media from Budapest and Bucharest against Ukrainian outlets.
- **Older villagers and urban young adults:** compare TV, radio, Viber and Telegram as sources by age in the consumption surveys.
- **Jewish communities, historical:** check how the Yiddish, Hebrew and Russian-language Jewish press of Odesa and Kyiv reached the shtetls.
- **Deaf and blind people:** check access to alerts and news in sign language and audio.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for media monitoring since 2014 and for press history; mixed for local news; thin for Krymchak, Karaim and Roma information networks.
- **Languages needed:** Church Slavonic for early print, Russian for Soviet newspapers and many channels, Crimean Tatar, Romanian, Yiddish for Odesa's early press, Ukrainian for monitors and surveys.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the Holodomor, Chornobyl, the Gongadze case and wartime information are contested ground: present each account as an account. Wartime reporting limits and media law go to Libra and Capricorn; Telegram channels may be anonymous and partisan.
- **Held for review:** nothing; anonymous channels were the one class weighed for exclusion, and they stay out as unverifiable, not under a standing rule.
