## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Volhynian and Polissian villages and Kyiv:** compare how children joined farm and household work and how early they were sent to school, in ethnographies and memoirs.
- **Hutsul and Boyko craft families:** check how carving, weaving and pottery were taught within the family, and how museum workshops changed it.
- **Crimean Tatars:** compare the medrese and new-method schools before 1944 with home and weekend teaching after the return.
- **Mennonite and German colonies (historical):** check their teacher training against nearby Ukrainian village schools in the same districts.
- **Jewish communities (historical):** compare kheyder, yeshiva and state school, and what girls learned, in memoirs from Podillia and Volhynia.
- **Bulgarians and Gagauz of the Budjak:** check whether home and church languages are taught at school or in Sunday classes.
- **Roma families:** check how children learn trades and language at home and how schools record them; sources are scarce, so begin with Roma NGOs.
- **Displaced children since 2014 and 2022:** compare learning online, in host schools and abroad, as comparison only for those outside Ukraine.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for school history and primers; mixed for learning in the home; thin for Karaim, Krymchak and Roma learning, where community memoirs are the likely route.
- **Languages needed:** Ruthenian and Church Slavonic for brotherhood schools, Arabic script and Ottoman Turkish for the medrese, German for the Zentralschule, Hebrew and Yiddish for kheyder memoirs.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** Soviet schooling, the language of instruction, and wartime schooling in occupied areas are contested ground; present each account as an account. Children's data needs care.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands; one pointer was set aside here under the standing rule.
