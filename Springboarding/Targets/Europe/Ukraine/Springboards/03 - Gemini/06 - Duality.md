## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Left Bank (Poltava, Chernihiv) and Right Bank (Vinnytsia, Zhytomyr):** compare how local histories, songs and museums tell the old split along the Dnipro.
- **Greek Catholics of Galicia and Orthodox of both jurisdictions:** check how each describes the Eastern-rite and Western-church tie, in their own catechisms and histories.
- **Russian-speaking Ukrainians of Kharkiv, Dnipro and Odesa:** check self-descriptions of holding two languages or identities in survey and memoir, without assigning one.
- **Bukovinian and Budjak communities who call themselves Moldovan or Romanian:** check which name each village uses, and when it changed.
- **Transcarpathian Hungarians and Rusyns:** compare double belonging across a border in their own press and organizations.
- **Karaim and Krymchaks:** check each community's own statements on Turkic language and Jewish or other faith; the sources are few, so start with community bodies.
- **Crimean Tatars:** compare ties to Crimea, Turkey and the mainland in their literature and memory projects.
- **Jewish communities (historical):** compare Yiddish, Hebrew and Russian or Ukrainian lives in Odesa's writers and the shtetl.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for church history, literature and identity surveys; mixed for folk dualism, where 19th-century collectors' framings need checking; thin for Karaim and Krymchak sources.
- **Languages needed:** Russian for Drahomanov's 1876 volume, Polish and Latin for early modern identity formulas, Turkish for Dağcı, Yiddish for An-sky, Ukrainian for church and survey sources.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** church jurisdictions, divided loyalty in wartime, the Karaim debate and dual citizenship in Transcarpathia are contested ground; present each account as an account, and send citizenship law to Libra.
