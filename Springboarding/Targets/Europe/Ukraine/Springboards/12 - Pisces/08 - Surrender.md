# Ukraine: Surrender (Pisces): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Ukraine, region Europe. **Language(s) for searching:** Ukrainian, Old East Slavic and Church Slavonic, the Russian of Ukraine, Crimean Tatar and Ottoman Turkish, Polish, German. **Concept in one line:** ceasing to resist; giving oneself, one's will or one's control over to something stronger or higher (God, fate, a lover, an enemy, illness, death), with release and letting go.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Galician towns and Left Bank towns:** check whether local memory frames the past surrenders (1245, 1775, 1939) as shame, prudence or betrayal.
- **Crimean Tatars:** check 1783 and 1944 together, and how each is sung and written.
- **Poles of Lviv and their Ukrainian neighbors:** check how 1939 is remembered by each.
- **Veterans' families and the Azovstal garrison's kin:** check how relatives describe yielding against what officials and commentators say.
- **Galician villages with insurgent pasts:** check family accounts of those who came in under amnesty and those who did not.
- **Orthodox (both jurisdictions) and Greek Catholic families at deathbeds:** check which prayers and customs of letting go each keeps.
- **Holodomor survivors' families across the center and the south:** check how giving up a child is told, if it is told at all.
- **Families who fled Kharkiv, Donetsk, Zaporizhzhia and Kherson oblasts:** check what they say about leaving homes and animals behind.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for military and political surrenders; mixed for illness and dying (rights reports, little ethnography); thin for surrender as religious practice outside the clergy's own writing.
- **Languages needed:** the chronicle's Old East Slavic for Danylo, Ottoman Turkish behind Fisher's sources, Polish for Lwów, German for 1941 camp records, Crimean Tatar for the songs.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** Azovstal, the amnesty files and 1941 captivity are open wounds; Soviet files carry the interrogators' words, not the prisoners'; famine testimony about children must be handled with care and without names unless published.
- **Held for review:** none. Western "fatalism" scales were weighed and kept out, because they would label a people before any study.
