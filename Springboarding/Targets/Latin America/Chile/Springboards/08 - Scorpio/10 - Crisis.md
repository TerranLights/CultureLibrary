*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Fishing families of the Biobío coast and Juan Fernández: check how tsunami memory sets when they run uphill.
- Concepción and Talcahuano residents: check memory of February 2010 and of the looting reports.
- Nitrate-era and copper families of the north: check how boom and bust are told within families.
- Norte Chico families of Copiapó and Chañaral: check flood memory in a desert.
- Small farmers and *huaso* families of Petorca and the Central Valley: check whether the dry years are spoken of as weather, as theft of water or as punishment.
- Santiago's *poblaciones*: check memories of 1982, the PEM and POJH, and the soup kitchens.
- Aymara and Quechua highland communities: check frost, drought and herd loss, and the rites to meet them.
- Rapa Nui: check how islanders answer the outside "collapse" story.
- Haitian families: check how the 2010 earthquake at home shapes fear and preparation here.
- Palestinian-Chilean families: check how a far-off catastrophe is lived at a distance.
- Pentecostal and Catholic congregations: check what a disaster is said to mean, and who opens the church as a shelter.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** earthquakes, tsunamis and recessions have deep records; personal and mental-health crises less so; Colla, Diaguita and Chango responses to hazard have few written sources.
- **Languages needed:** technical Spanish for seismic and hydrological reports; Aymara for highland hazards; Rapa Nui for the collapse debate; Kreyòl for Haitian families' memory.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** 1972 to 1973 and 2019 are contested ground; read each side as an account. Looting reports can stigmatize districts; suicide data are personal.
- **Held for review:** the 1960 Valdivia earthquake was weighed as the country's largest; it struck lands now in Araucanía-Patagonia, so it is kept for comparison only.
