## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Residents of Santiago's west and of its high eastern *comunas*: check where each says the city's line runs.
- Aymara communities of Tarapacá and Parinacota: check whether village edges matter more than the national one.
- Lickanantay and tourism operators of San Pedro: check how a closed gate is explained to each side.
- *Huaso* families: check how hedges, ditches and gates mark a *fundo*, and the customs about crossing them.
- Palestinian- and Croatian-descended families: check who marries out and how each generation speaks of it.
- University students and their parents: check where each generation puts the line on touch and comment.
- Haitian families renting single rooms: check how privacy is kept inside shared walls.
- Rapa Nui families: check the words for outsiders and what each allows them.
- Fishing families of Juan Fernández and of the Biobío coves: check how sea plots are claimed and respected.
- Mapuche families in Santiago: check the edge between city life and kin ties to the south, which is comparison only.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich on segregation and institutional protocols; mixed on touch and greeting; scarce on Andean land markers and island customs.
- **Languages needed:** Spanish; Aymara for territory words; Rapa Nui for land and belonging; Arabic for family terms.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the Biobío frontier and the northern lines after the War of the Pacific are contested ground, and this sheet settles none of them. Clergy abuse involves living survivors. Lickanantay and Rapa Nui decisions on access are theirs.
