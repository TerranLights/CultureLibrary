# Chile: Escapism (Pisces): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Copper workers on long shifts and their families in Calama: check what the rest week is for, as each side says.
- Young people of Santiago's *poblaciones* and of private-school *comunas*: check which escapes each can pay for and which draw punishment.
- Aymara families of the highland fiesta and their Pentecostal kin: check how each draws the line between libation and drunkenness.
- Fishing families of the Biobío coves: check where men go when the sea is closed.
- *Huaso* families: check whether the *fonda* and the rodeo are rest, release or excess in their own words.
- Haitian and Venezuelan newcomers: check what leisure looks like in the first years, and where it is found.
- Rapa Nui islanders: check how they speak of mainlanders who come to "escape" to the island.
- Juan Fernández islanders: check how a castaway legend shapes what visitors seek.
- Evangelical converts: check how the break from drink is told in testimonies.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich in drug and alcohol surveys and festival records; mixed on media habits; thin for Indigenous and island practice, where ceremony must not be treated as escape.
- **Languages needed:** Spanish slang and broadside verse; Aymara and Quechua drinking words; Kreyòl for newcomers' leisure.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** Aymara and Lickanantay libations are ceremony, described as communities choose; street children and drug users are vulnerable; the 1996 escape is contested.
- **Held for review:** binge-watching and gaming studies made abroad, with only a thin Chilean subsample, are not listed until a Chilean study turns up; nothing is carried elsewhere.
