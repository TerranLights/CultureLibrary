# México: Care (Cancer): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** México, region Latin America. **Language(s) for searching:** Mexican Spanish (health, domestic-work and folk-healing registers); classical Nahuatl; Tsotsil; Nahua and Teenek of the Huasteca for domestic workers' own accounts; Ñuu Savi and Zapotec for care across migration. **Concept in one line:** attending to the needs and well-being of another, above all one who can't meet them alone: the attitude, the work and the relationship.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Monterrey and Saltillo employers against Huasteca Nahua and Teenek workers:** check care work seen from both sides of the household.
- **Mexico City's middle class against the capital's *colonias populares*:** check paid against kin care for elders.
- **Nuevo León and Coahuila *fidencista* communities:** check healing as care in the north.
- **Tsotsil and Tseltal highland towns:** check the *j'ilol*'s place beside clinics.
- **Ñuu Savi and Zapotec towns with many emigrants:** check who cares for elders left behind.
- **Veracruz towns on the freight line:** check groups that feed and nurse migrants.
- **Disabled people and their organizations:** check self-advocacy against charity models.
- **Spanish, Lebanese and Jewish community institutions:** check community hospitals and aid societies as their own care systems.
- **Mennonite colonies:** check whether colonies run their own clinics and elder care.
- **Afro-Mexican towns of the Costa Chica:** check healers and care of elders; sources are few, so begin with Guerrero and Oaxaca universities.
- **Comparison only, because care systems and the people who move between them cross these edges:** Yucatán (Maya healers and clinics of the Peninsula), Sonora (Yoeme and Comcaac healing), Cascadia's Bajas (Tijuana's clinics for foreign patients; Kumiai elders), the CSA (Tejano *curanderismo*) and the diaspora (women caring for families in the United States while kin care for their own children at home). Where México ends is for the author.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for unpaid-care accounts, health programs and domestic work; mixed for folk healing; thin for Kikapú and Afro-Mexican care and for men as carers.
- **Languages needed:** Spanish; classical Nahuatl and colonial Spanish for Farfán; Tsotsil; Huasteca Nahuatl and Teenek for workers' testimony.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** patients' and care-home records; healing knowledge some healers keep private; attacks on health workers in 2020; health and labor law go to Libra and Capricorn.
- **Held for review:** none; no carer, healer or health-program pointer here met a standing rule.
