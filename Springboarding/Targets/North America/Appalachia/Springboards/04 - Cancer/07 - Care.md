# Appalachia: Care (Cancer): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Appalachia, region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** Appalachian English; Eastern Band Cherokee (syllabary); Pennsylvania German; Spanish. **Concept in one line:** attending to the needs and well-being of another, above all one who can't meet them alone: the attitude, the work and the relationship.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Central Appalachia's coalfields:** union-built care against county clinics and church help, and how families cope with black lung and overdose.
- **Pittsburgh and the Ohio Valley:** fraternal benefit societies and parish nursing against present-day hospital systems.
- **Southern Appalachia's retirement counties** (around Asheville, Blairsville, Hiawassee): newcomer retirees' care networks against local families' kin care.
- **Eastern Band of Cherokee:** the Band's own health system and community care, without trespassing on restricted healing knowledge.
- **Black Appalachians:** church nurse units, lodges and funeral homes as care institutions under segregation and after.
- **Amish and Mennonite communities:** church-district aid and home care, from their own writings.
- **Latino newcomers:** promotoras, clinics and the care sent home as remittances, in Dalton, Gainesville and western North Carolina.
- **Lebanese and Syrian families** of Charleston and Wheeling: look at Orthodox and Maronite parish visiting and who nursed elders at home.
- **Melungeon families:** sources on care are scarce; look in county oral histories rather than general studies.
- **Out-migrants** returning to care for parents: diaspora, comparison only.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for health institutions and folk medicine; mixed for unpaid care at home (state-level only); thin for newcomer and Melungeon caregiving in their own words.
- **Languages needed:** Appalachian English healers' vocabulary; Cherokee, with the Band; Pennsylvania German for Braucherei; Spanish for clinics and promotoras.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** Cherokee medicine formulas collected by outsiders (Mooney, 1891) and healers' knowledge are not for open use; ask the Band first. Health records are confidential. Coverage of free-clinic lines has drawn outside poverty imagery; read patients' own accounts first.
- **Held for review:** no healing or care-institution source was excluded.
