# Appalachia: Peace at any price (Libra): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Appalachia, region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** Appalachian English (with its Scots, Ulster and English layers), Cherokee (Eastern Band), German and Pennsylvania German, Spanish. **Concept in one line:** keeping peace or harmony by giving way, staying silent or paying whatever it costs, even when the cost is one's own (or someone else's) rights, truth or justice.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Central Appalachian coal counties:** compare safety complaints filed with disasters that followed, to see where danger went unreported.
- **Northern Appalachia** (Aliquippa, the Monongahela towns, the anthracite counties): check company-union years against later union records.
- **Shenandoah Valley:** compare Mennonite, Brethren and Quaker answers to each war's draft.
- **Southern Appalachia and the Blue Ridge:** check court records for protective orders sought and withdrawn.
- **Eastern Band of Cherokee:** read the Band's own telling of the Treaty Party and of Tsali before outside histories.
- **Black Appalachians:** compare what interviewees say they did not report with what newspapers printed.
- **Amish and Mennonite settlements** of Appalachian Ohio and Pennsylvania: check how far nonresistance reaches into lawsuits and police reports.
- **Italian, Slovak, Polish and Hungarian** families: check what disaster widows accepted from companies, and whether consuls pressed for more.
- **Latino newcomers:** check crime-reporting rates and U visa use in poultry and nursery towns.
- **Melungeon families:** check accounts of hiding origins to keep peace with neighbors; sources thin.
- **Out-migrants** (comparison only): whether migrants' oral histories describe keeping quiet at city workplaces.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for treaty, labor and draft law; mixed for family silence (fiction, memoir and oral history more than records); thin for newcomers' reporting.
- **Languages needed:** Cherokee for the 1829 law's syllabary text and the Band's own accounts; German script for Valley Mennonite records; Spanish for crime-victim services.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** abuse and protective-order records are sealed or private; survivors' accounts need consent. The Treaty Party's killing in 1839 is still debated among the Cherokee nations: present each side as its own.
- **Held for review:** no pointer on giving way or keeping quiet was set aside under the standing rules.

## 5. First queries
1. Read the Treaty of New Echota and the Cherokee law of 1829 side by side; note the signers and the penalty.
2. Search the Confederate statutes of 1862 for the exemption clause; note the fee and who qualified.
3. Swarthmore College Peace Collection: search "Civilian Public Service" with Virginia and North Carolina camps.
4. Read *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin*; list what the record says about Aliquippa.
5. Search MSHA's discrimination complaint decisions under Section 105(c) for Kentucky and West Virginia mines.
