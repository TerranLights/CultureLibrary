# Appalachia: Death and transformation, the domain and its folkways (Scorpio): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Appalachia, region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** Appalachian English; Cherokee (Eastern Band, syllabary); Pennsylvania German; Spanish; for old parish and lodge books, the Italian, Polish, Slovak, Rusyn, Hungarian, Welsh and Arabic of coal and steel towns. **Concept in one line:** how a culture faces death, power and what is hidden: death and rebirth, sex ways, shared resources, inheritance and secrets.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Eastern Kentucky and southern West Virginia coalfields against Pittsburgh, the Mon Valley and Appalachian Ohio: check whether churches, union locals, lodges or funeral homes keep the dead, since each leaves different records.
- The Eastern Band of Cherokee (Qualla Boundary, Snowbird): check what the Band publishes on death and renewal and what it reserves.
- Old Regular, Primitive, Universalist and Holiness congregations: check each body's teaching on the afterlife and on being changed.
- Black Appalachians in McDowell County camps and in Knoxville and Asheville: check separate cemeteries and burial societies that white-centered collections can miss.
- Melungeon families of Hancock County, Tennessee: check the Melungeon Heritage Association on contested, once-hidden origins.
- Italian, Polish, Slovak, Rusyn, Welsh and Lebanese parishes: check Catholic, Byzantine and Orthodox memorial rites beside the Protestant majority.
- Amish and Mennonite settlements of Appalachian Ohio and Pennsylvania: check plain funerals and church discipline.
- Latino newcomers in Dalton and Gainesville, Georgia: check wakes and repatriation.
- Out-migrants in Midwestern cities (comparison only): check whether bodies came home for burial.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for burial customs, mining deaths, mountain religion and ballads; mixed for sex ways and pooled money; thin for Black, Melungeon and Latino mourning. The nation's exact lands are still to be settled; a source's own definition (the Appalachian Regional Commission's counties, for example) stays that source's, not the nation's units.
- **Languages needed:** Kurrent German script for Moravian and Pennsylvania German records; Slavic and Italian for lodge ledgers; Cherokee readers through the Band.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** Cherokee medicine and burial places are the Band's to share; overdose and suicide records touch living families; feud and snake-handling imagery largely comes from outside writers.
- **Held for review:** None of the death, burial, lodge or land sources gathered for this overview needed holding back.

## 5. First queries
1. Read *Decoration Day in the Mountains*, then the Brown Collection's death-belief entries, noting each county.
2. Search the finding aids of East Tennessee State University's Archives of Appalachia and Berea College's Special Collections for "funeral", "cemetery", "wake" and "memorial meeting".
3. In CDC WONDER, pull underlying cause of death for the Commission's Appalachian counties against the rest of each state, 1999 to the latest year.
4. Ask the Kituwah Preservation and Education Program which published materials on death and mourning it recommends to outsiders.
5. Search the American Folklife Center's online collections for "Coal River" with "cemetery" and "ramps".
