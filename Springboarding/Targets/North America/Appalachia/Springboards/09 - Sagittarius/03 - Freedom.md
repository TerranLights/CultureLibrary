# Appalachia: Freedom (Sagittarius): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Appalachia, region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** Appalachian English, including 19th-century Black and white usage; Cherokee of the syllabary press; Pennsylvania German; Spanish; Hungarian, Polish, Slovak and Welsh for the freedom days of immigrant lodges. **Concept in one line:** being able to act, speak, believe and move without constraint by others or by the state, and freedom as a belief and a political ideal.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Black Appalachians (east Tennessee, Pittsburgh, the coal camps):** check emancipation days and the Black press for when freedom is spoken of as won, and by whom.
- **Eastern Band of Cherokee:** check how the Band's own sources state freedom as sovereignty, and how Removal and the families who stayed are remembered.
- **Coal-camp families of Central Appalachia:** check company-town records and union papers for what workers said they could do, before and after the union.
- **Scots-Irish-, English- and German-descended farm families of the Blue Ridge and the Valley:** check deeds, letters and sermons for whether freedom is tied to owning land; Fischer's claim is a frame to test, not a fact.
- **Mennonite, Brethren and Amish communities:** check their own histories for freedom of conscience, especially in wartime.
- **Hungarian, Polish, Slovak and Welsh lodges:** check commemorations and the ethnic press for which homeland freedom stories were kept.
- **Latino newcomers in poultry and carpet towns:** check worker-center and parish records for how documents and immigration enforcement shape freedom of movement; handle with care.
- **Out-migrants in Midwestern cities (diaspora, comparison only):** check whether leaving is described as escape or as exile.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for abolition, labor and civil rights history; mixed for present attitudes; thin on Cherokee- and Spanish-language accounts.
- **Languages needed:** Appalachian English across two centuries, from *free papers* to union speech; the Cherokee syllabary for the *Phoenix*'s Removal debates; Pennsylvania German for nonresistant sects' wartime papers; Spanish for worker centers; Hungarian, Polish, Slovak and Welsh for homeland freedom days.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** slavery narratives and Removal are painful histories, to be read with descendants' own accounts; immigration status of living people must stay private. Any question of what the law permits goes to Libra (letter) or Capricorn (spirit).
- **Held for review:** the emancipation, labor and Removal sources raised no case under the standing rules; none were withheld.

1. Search "Born in Slavery" for narrators from mountain counties of Tennessee, Kentucky and North Carolina, and list how each speaks of freedom.
2. Find a reprint of Embree's *The Emancipator* and list its arguments for freedom.
3. Filter the Cooperative Election Study by county to compare rights items in coalfield, northern and southern Appalachian counties.
4. Search Network to Freedom for listed sites in Appalachian Ohio and western Pennsylvania.
5. Ask Pittsburgh's Hungarian and Polish lodges for programs of their March 15 and May 3 observances.
