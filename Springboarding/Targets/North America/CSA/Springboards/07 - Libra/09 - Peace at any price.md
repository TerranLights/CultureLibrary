# CSA: Peace at any price (Libra): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** CSA, region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** political and pulpit English of compromise; Black Southern sayings; Cajun French; Tejano Spanish of LULAC's era; Miami exile Spanish; Vietnamese proverbs and talk radio; Muscogee for the town councils of the 1700s. **Concept in one line:** keeping peace or harmony by giving way, staying silent or paying whatever it costs, even when the cost is one's own (or someone else's) rights, truth or justice.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **White moderates in Atlanta, Birmingham and Little Rock:** check how business slogans ("a city too busy to hate") and clergy appeals framed calm as the goal.
- **Black leaders and congregations from 1895 to 1965:** compare accommodation and protest as strategies, in each leader's own words.
- **Cherokee factions in Georgia, 1835-39:** read the Treaty Party's and the majority's own arguments side by side.
- **Tejano LULAC members and Raza Unida organizers in Crystal City and Corpus Christi:** compare what each would give up for peace with Anglo power.
- **Cuban Miami's hard-line and dialogue camps:** check who spoke and who kept quiet after the bombings of the 1970s and 1980s.
- **Southern Baptist, Catholic and Vietnamese parish communities:** compare how each handled abuse claims and family quarrels.
- **Quakers and other peace churches in North Carolina:** check what refusing to fight cost them, as a contrast to giving way.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for compromise politics, civil-rights law and Civil War memory; mixed for church silences (recent reporting, few long records); thin for family conflict avoidance among Vietnamese and Cuban households (needs interviews and radio archives).
- **Languages needed:** Spanish for the 1930s Tejano press on accommodation and for exile radio; Vietnamese for call-in shows; Muscogee for the 1718 council as the nation tells it; French for Cajun sayings about trouble.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the 1877 settlement, the Atlanta address and Cherokee removal are contested, so give each side its own voice; abuse survivors and accused persons are living people; exile violence in Miami is recent and named persons only as courts found.
- **Held for review:** South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands; how they keep peace with neighbors and employers is not scouted here.
