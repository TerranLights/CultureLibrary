# CSA: Surrender (Pisces): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** CSA, region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** Southern American English; African American English; Tejano Spanish; Vietnamese; Cajun French; Muscogee and Mikasuki only through the nations' own histories. **Concept in one line:** ceasing to resist; giving oneself, one's will or one's control over to something stronger or higher (God, fate, a lover, an enemy, illness, death); release and letting go.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **White Southerners with Confederate family memory and Black Southerners for whom 1865 meant freedom:** check how each family tells "the Surrender", never merging the two.
- **Seminole, Miccosukee and Muscogee-descended communities:** compare how each nation tells defeat, holdout and survival.
- **Gulf Coast Vietnamese:** check what Black April means to those who lived 1975 and to their U.S.-born grandchildren.
- **South Texas Tejanos:** check end-of-life talk in hospital, parish and home, without assuming one stance.
- **Evangelical and Catholic congregations:** compare the altar call with confession and last rites as practices of yielding.
- **Coastal Texas and Louisiana residents:** compare who leaves and who stays before storms, and the reasons each gives.
- **Lumbee and Black Belt churchgoers:** check prayer language of handing over illness and debt in sermons and testimony.
- **Cajun Acadiana:** test whether *c'est la vie* signals resignation, humor or both, in speakers' own explanation.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for war memory, hospice and labor data; mixed for religious yielding beyond revival records; thin for Native accounts outside each nation's own histories.
- **Languages needed:** Vietnamese for Black April speeches; Spanish for Valley end-of-life outreach; Muscogee and Mikasuki only via the nations; French for Acadiana phrases.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** Appomattox and the Lost Cause carry contested memory: set Black, white and Native accounts side by side; the "fatalism" label has been used against Black and Latino patients; suicide must not be framed as surrender without care.
- **Held for review:** acceptance and renunciation teachings brought by South Asian-heritage communities in the nation's lands.
