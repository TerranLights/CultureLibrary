*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Nuche of Ignacio and Towaoc:** check tribal radio schedules and newsletters for which matters go out in Ute, in English, or both.
- **Hinóno'éí and Tsėhésenėstsestȯtse in the Colorado record:** check who spoke for them at councils and through which interpreters, since the record is mostly in others' hands.
- **San Luis Valley Hispanos:** check proverb collections and recordings for norms of speaking to elders, and compare with Mexican-born neighbors' usage.
- **Denver and Pueblo Chicanos:** check recorded speeches and poetry readings of the 1960s and 70s as a public-speech genre.
- **Newer Mexican and Central American residents:** check clinic and school interpreter records for language brokering by children.
- **Amache generation and descendants:** check interviews for how wartime pressure shaped the use of Japanese at home.
- **Southern coalfield and Pueblo mill families (Italian, Slovene, Greek, Mexican):** check company and union papers for how meaning crossed languages at work.
- **Aurora's Ethiopian, Eritrean and Somali residents:** check community radio, church and mosque announcements for register and language choice.
- **Colorado Springs evangelicals and military families:** check counseling and chaplaincy materials for how "communication" is framed in family life.
- **Deaf Coloradans:** check Deaf club and school histories for ASL use and access.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for public speech, broadcasting and Deaf access; mixed for everyday conversation, which needs recordings; thin for Ute and Somali norms of talk in print.
- **Languages needed:** Spanish for the proverb and oratory sources; Japanese for Amache-era bilingual records; Amharic for "wax and gold" speech; ASL for Deaf community sources.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** treaty-council records are one-sided accounts; Ute and Arapaho speech norms are the nations' to describe. The legal side of language access and the state's 1988 official-English amendment go to Libra.
- **Held for review:** nothing set aside; a nationwide business-culture directness guide was weighed and dropped as country-level, not under a rule.
