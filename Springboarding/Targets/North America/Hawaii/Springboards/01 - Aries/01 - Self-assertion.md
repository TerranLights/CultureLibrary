# Hawaii: Self-assertion (Aries): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** Pidgin and island English first for everyday speech; ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi; Japanese, Ilocano, Tagalog, Korean, Cantonese, Portuguese; Samoan, Tongan, Chuukese. **Concept in one line:** declaring and acting on one's own existence, wishes and claims in the presence of others.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli in homestead communities (Nānākuli, Waimānalo, Hoʻolehua on Molokaʻi) and in Honolulu offices: check whether speaking up to outsiders and inside the ʻohana follow the same rules.
- Japanese and Okinawan Americans by generation: compare how restraint and speaking out are described by Issei, Nisei and Sansei, and whether Okinawan families describe it apart from *Naichi* families.
- Filipino residents by origin (Ilocano, Visayan, Tagalog-speaking newer arrivals): check whether labor-era accounts of speaking up match those of the post-1965 migrants.
- Samoan and Tongan congregations: check who may speak at church and family gatherings, and in what order.
- Marshallese and Chuukese residents: check how they appear in public testimony and community meetings, a recent and thinly documented record.
- Haole residents (long-settled against recent movers) and military families: check how complaint and directness are discussed in local media when they and island-raised residents meet.
- Women and men across groups: compare labor and political leaders' accounts.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for labor history and political protest; mixed for everyday speech norms, where KEEP and a few ethnographies carry much of the load; thin for Micronesian and Niʻihau communities.
- **Languages needed:** Pidgin is central for everyday talk; Hawaiian for nūpepa and moʻolelo; Ilocano for strike-era papers; Japanese for prewar newspapers.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the overthrow, sovereignty claims and land struggles are contested; present each account as an account. Testimony lists name private residents: cite records, not individuals.
- **Held for review:** none; the speech-norm and labor sources weighed here raised nothing under the standing rules.

## 5. First queries
1. Ulukau: search the Fornander Collection for "hoʻopāpā" and read the contest passages.
