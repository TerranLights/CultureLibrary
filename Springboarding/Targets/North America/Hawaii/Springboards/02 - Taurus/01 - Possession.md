# Hawaii: Possession (Taurus): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi for possessive grammar and old notices; Pidgin and island English for everyday stuff; Japanese, Tagalog and Ilocano for things carried and sent; Samoan and Tongan for valuables that circulate. **Concept in one line:** having and holding things: the bond between people and what they keep, use, carry, store, lend, lose and give away.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli families on Hawaiian Home Lands (Papakōlea, Nānākuli, Keaukaha, Hoʻolehua): check which family objects stay with a lease house and which travel with kin.
- Niʻihau families and the Niʻihau shell-lei makers of Kauaʻi: check, through the makers and their own statements, whether a lei is kept, sold or given.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: compare keepsakes of the dead and what was destroyed in 1941.
- Filipino (Ilocano, Visayan) households: separate goods kept in the islands from goods sent in boxes.
- Chinese families of Chinatown and Mānoa: check heirlooms brought from Zhongshan against things bought here.
- Portuguese families of Kalihi, Hilo and Upcountry Maui: compare keepsakes from Azores and Madeira with those made locally.
- Samoan, Tongan, Marshallese and Chuukese households: separate valuables given at events from personal belongings; Micronesian sources are thin, look to community associations.
- Military families: check what moves with each posting and what stays in storage.
- Unsheltered residents in Kakaʻako, Waiʻanae and Hilo: check what people keep when the city clears encampments, in their own words.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for museum objects, nūpepa notices and probate inventories; mixed for living households; thin for Micronesian and Niʻihau belongings.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian to read notices and probate testimony; Tagalog and Ilocano for shipping and household talk; Samoan and Tongan for gift terms.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** *moepū* and *iwi kūpuna* are restricted; work through lineal descendants and recognized organizations, never around them. Methods of making Niʻihau shell lei may be family-held; ask the makers what may be recorded.
- **Held for review:** household altars and memorial tablets kept in Japanese and Okinawan homes as Buddhist practice, held as a category only.

## 5. First queries
1. Papakilo: search "nalowale" in nūpepa, 1860–1890; tabulate the kinds of goods named.
2. UH library catalog: Wilson "possessive markers" Hawaiian; then Elbert-Pukui's chapter on possession.
3. Hawaii State Archives: ask for probate inventory series for one Oʻahu and one Hawaiʻi Island district.
4. Library catalog: "Hawaiian calabash" Jenkins and "Ka lei" McDonald.
5. *Crime in Hawaii*: download the latest report; note property crime by county.
