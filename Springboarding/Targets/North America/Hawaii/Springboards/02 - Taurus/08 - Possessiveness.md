# Hawaii: Possessiveness (Taurus): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi for jealousy in old narratives; Pidgin and island English for turf and "locals only"; Japanese, Korean and Cantonese for jealousy idioms; Tagalog, Ilocano and Portuguese for jealous love in song; Samoan and Tongan for partners and family claims. **Concept in one line:** holding on to what one sees as one's own, people included, beyond what the bond or the thing warrants, and the urge to keep others away from it.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- North Shore, Waiʻanae and Windward surf communities: compare how each speaks of who may ride its breaks, and when visitors are tolerated.
- Kānaka Maoli fishing and gathering families (Miloliʻi, Hāʻena, Kaʻū, Molokaʻi): check how family spots are kept and shared, as the families describe it.
- Niʻihau families: check how their community limits outside access, in its own words, before reading outsiders' accounts.
- Japanese and Okinawan families: compare *yakimochi* in family talk with how jealousy is shown in community humor.
- Filipino (Ilocano, Visayan) families: check jealousy in courtship and marriage in Ilocano songs and community radio.
- Portuguese and Puerto Rican families: check jealous love in their music and feast-day courtship.
- Samoan and Tongan families: check how partners and families claim each other at church and at events.
- Marshallese and Chuukese residents: check how controlling behavior is named in their languages; sources are scarce, look to health workers.
- Military families: check deployment and jealousy in base counseling programs.
- Haole newcomers: check how they meet localism at beaches and in neighborhoods.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for surf localism and place disputes; mixed for partner control (small state samples); thin for Micronesian and Niʻihau families.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for 19th-century narratives; Ilocano and Tagalog for song and radio; Samoan and Tongan for church counseling.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** surf and gang turf can mean violence; do not single out any group. Fishing spots and gathering places may be family-held; ask before recording. Partner-violence data need care.
- **Held for review:** nothing set aside; the jealousy and turf pointers come from narrative, surf, fishing and social-service sources, none of them under a standing rule.
