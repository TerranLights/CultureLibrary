# Hawaii: Communication (Gemini): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** Pidgin and island English for everyday talk; ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi for oratory and chant; Japanese, Uchinaaguchi, Korean and Cantonese for reticence and address; Ilocano and Tagalog; Samoan and Tongan for formal speech; Marshallese and Chuukese for clinic and school contact. **Concept in one line:** the exchange of meaning between people, by speech, writing, gesture, silence and media, and the ideas held about what good communication is and what it is for.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli speakers in Hawaiian-medium and homestead communities (Nānākuli, Hoʻolehua on Molokaʻi, Keaukaha in Hilo): check how formal speech, chant and casual talk divide by setting.
- Niʻihau speakers and Kekaha families: check which talk their own school and church make public, and leave the rest with them.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: compare reticence and greeting forms across generations, issei letters to sansei texting.
- Korean and Chinese families: check address forms in church and association meetings, Cantonese and Hakka apart.
- Filipino households (Ilocano, Visayan, Tagalog), Portuguese and Puerto Rican families: check respect forms used with elders and how far they survive in island English.
- Samoan, Tongan, Marshallese and Chuukese communities: check formal oration at church and family events against everyday talk; Micronesian sources are scarce.
- Haole residents, military families and paniolo families of Waimea and Upcountry Maui: check how directness, eye contact and greeting are taught to or kept by each.
- Hilo and Kona, Lahaina and Hāna, Lānaʻi City: compare small-town talk with Honolulu's.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for Pidgin and Hawaiian oratory; mixed for conversation norms across groups; thin for Micronesian and Niʻihau speech and for sign and gesture outside the shaka.
- **Languages needed:** Pidgin and Hawaiian above all; Japanese for reticence studies; Samoan and Tongan for oration; Chuukese and Marshallese with community interpreters.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** entry chants and hālau protocol belong to their teachers; do not record or publish outside their terms. Studies of Pidgin speakers can stigmatize; read them with the speakers' own writing.
- **Held for review:** none; the clinic, broadcast and grammar sources weighed for talk itself raised no item to set aside.
