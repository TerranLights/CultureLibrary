*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli readers of the nūpepa and of *Ka Wai Ola* today: check how sources of news shifted after the Hawaiian press ended, island by island.
- Niʻihau's community: check what outside reporting exists, and set it against what the community itself chooses to release.
- Japanese and Okinawan, Korean and Chinese families: compare reliance on the community press against the English dailies, especially before 1945.
- Filipino households (Ilocano, Visayan, Tagalog): check homeland radio and newspapers against island media.
- Samoan, Tongan, Marshallese and Chuukese communities: check church announcements, radio programs and phone networks as channels; written sources are scarce, so look in community groups first.
- Military families: check base newspapers and command messages, Red Hill updates included, against civilian media.
- Rural districts (Kaʻū, Hāna, Molokaʻi, Lānaʻi, the North Shore): check where small weeklies or none at all serve, and Puna's lava-alert networks.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for the press and official statistics; mixed for trust in news by group; thin for Micronesian channels and for anything from Niʻihau.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for the nūpepa (most never translated); Korean and Japanese for pre-1945 papers; Ilocano for the Filipino press.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** genealogies and some Hale Nauā material are family- or society-held. Health data that singles out groups can stigmatize; read the data-governance arguments with it. The 2023 warning accounts are contested.
- **Held for review:** none; the press, archive and data sources listed here are civic or secular, and no religious-community records were needed for news itself.
