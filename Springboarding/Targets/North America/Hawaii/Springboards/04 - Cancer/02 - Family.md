## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli families of Kaʻū, Hāna and Waiʻanae: separate family by genealogy from family by *hānai* and by shared land.
- Niʻihau families in Kekaha and Puʻuwai: ask only through the community's school and spokespeople, since family knowledge there is held closely.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: compare the *ie* headship with the Okinawan *munchū*; find out which line was kept in the islands.
- Chinese and Korean families: weigh surname and village associations against the household as the unit of family.
- Filipino families (Ilocano and Visayan): look at ninong-ninang sponsorship and sponsorship of relatives' migration.
- Lines of Azorean, Madeiran and Puerto Rican descent (Kula, Pāʻia, Hilo): find out whether godchildren and in-laws sit at the family table as full kin.
- Samoan, Tongan, Marshallese and Chuukese families: test how far kin duty reaches at life events; Micronesian clan sources are scarce, start with community groups.
- Haole, mixed and military families: ask who counts as "calabash" kin when blood relatives are on the continent; paniolo families of Waimea and Upcountry add ranch lines.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** genealogy and adoption are strongly sourced in Hawaiian and in print; kin duty in plantation-era groups sits in community histories; Micronesian clan ties are hardly written up.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for genealogies and the Board of Genealogy papers; Cantonese for surname associations; Ilocano for sponsorship terms.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** genealogies (*moʻokūʻauhau*) and chiefly lines are family property and still disputed in sovereignty and trust cases; use what families have published and ask before going further. Kinship disputes over land and inheritance go to Libra and Capricorn as law, and to Scorpio as inheritance.
- **Held for review:** nothing; the kin pointers weighed here (surname associations, Korean congregations, Hawaiian church funerals) all fall inside what the sheet may name.
