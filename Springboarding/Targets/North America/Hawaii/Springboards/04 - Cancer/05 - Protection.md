## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli: separate *ʻaumakua* protection kept by families (ʻEwa, Kaʻū, Molokaʻi) from protection claimed for land by *kiaʻi*.
- Niʻihau: the island's closure is presented by its owners and residents as protecting the community; take that account from them, and others from their critics.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: compare *omamori* from shrines with *shīsā* on rooftops in Hilo and Wahiawā.
- Chinese and Korean households: ask what charms and door images are kept, and by whom.
- Filipino families of Waipahu and Kahului: ask whether *anting-anting* and saints' images are still kept.
- Portuguese and Puerto Rican families: find which saints are asked for protection at home and at feasts.
- Pacific Islander families in Kalihi, Kona and Hilo (Samoan, Tongan, Marshallese, Chuukese): in each group's own words, note who is expected to step in when a relative is threatened, and which church or leader is called; written Micronesian sources are very few.
- Haole, military and paniolo households: compare locks, dogs and guns on ranches and bases with town neighborhoods.
- Puna and Kaʻū lava zones, Lahaina, and Kauaʻi's north shore: set protection from disaster against protection from people.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** state program data and moʻolelo of guardians are both strong; household protective habits are hardly surveyed; Micronesian practice is barely recorded.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for guardian traditions and *ʻaumakua* terms; Japanese for shrine practice; Marshallese and Chuukese for pandemic-era community reports.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** *ʻaumakua* are family matters, often private; ask families, never publish lists. The military's role and Mauna Kea's *kiaʻi* are contested; give each side. Gun and child-welfare law belong to Libra and Capricorn.
- **Held for review:** nothing; the guardians, charms and programs on this sheet come from Hawaiian, Shinto, Filipino, Korean and state sources, and none called for holding.
