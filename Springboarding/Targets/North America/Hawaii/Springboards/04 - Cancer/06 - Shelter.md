*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi; Pidgin and island English; Japanese; Cantonese; Korean; Tagalog and Ilocano; Portuguese; Samoan and Tongan; Marshallese and Chuukese through outreach workers. **Concept in one line:** cover and refuge from danger and the elements: a roof, a safe place for those who have none, and the duty to give it.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli: compare beach and valley camps (Waiʻanae, Waimānalo, Mākua) with Home Lands; ask who calls it shelter, who calls it homelessness, and who calls it return to land.
- Niʻihau: shelter on and off the island is a community matter; take it only from the community.
- Japanese, Okinawan and Chinese descendants: compare elders housed in shelters or care with the family taking them in.
- Korean and Filipino families: check doubling up in Kalihi and Waipahu as shelter given to new arrivals.
- Portuguese and Puerto Rican families: ask how relatives were housed after plantation camps closed.
- Micronesian residents (Chuukese, Marshallese): with outreach workers, gather their own accounts of shelters, doubling up and camps; the literature is thin and often written about them.
- Samoan and Tongan families: count church halls as shelter after fires and evictions.
- Haole newcomers and veterans: compare arrivals who end up unhoused with long-time residents; military families add base housing as cover.
- Lahaina against Puna's lava evacuees: compare two kinds of disaster shelter on two islands.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** shelter counts and service reports are plentiful; older shelter practice rests on material-culture surveys and travelers; unhoused people's own accounts are scattered.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for house and cave terms; Chuukese and Marshallese for shelter users; Japanese for evacuation-era records in Hilo.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** caves and rock shelters often hold *iwi kūpuna*; never publish locations, and go through burial councils and families. Encampment sweeps, beach camps and Lahaina recovery are contested; set out each account.
- **Held for review:** nothing held; the shelters and studies weighed here are Christian, civic, Hawaiian or secular, and none fell under the South Asian or Buddhism rules.

## 5. First queries
1. Library catalog: Buck "Arts and crafts of Hawaii"; read the chapters on houses and shelters.

4. News archives: "compassionate disruption" Honolulu, with residents' statements.
5. Search "Ka Laʻi Ola" Lahaina; list who runs it and who may live there.
