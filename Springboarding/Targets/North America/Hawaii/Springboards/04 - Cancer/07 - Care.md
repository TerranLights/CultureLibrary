## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli: compare care by *kahuna lapaʻau* and *lomilomi* practitioners with clinic care, in Waiʻanae, Hāna and Hoʻolehua.
- Niʻihau's families: any account of care there comes from the community and its liaisons in Kekaha.
- Nisei and sansei children of Japanese and Okinawan families in Mōʻiliʻili, Hilo and Wahiawā: ask who moved a parent into Kuakini or a nursing home, and who kept the parent at home, and why.
- Chinese and Korean families: compare benevolent societies' care for the sick and the dead with family care.
- Filipino families: separate care-home operators from families caring for their own elders, and from nurses.
- Azorean, Madeiran and Puerto Rican descendants in Hilo and Upcountry Maui: trace parish women's sodalities that visited and fed the sick.
- Pacific Islander households (Samoan, Tongan, Marshallese, Chuukese): through community health workers, hear who nurses an ailing parent and when a clinic is trusted; almost nothing on Micronesian family care is in print.
- Haole retirees, military families and paniolo families: compare paid care and veterans' care with ranch families caring at home far from hospitals.
- Neighbor islands against Honolulu: compare care where the nearest specialist is a flight away.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** institutional care is well recorded; family care and healing practice are known through a few books and practitioners; Micronesian care is thinly recorded.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for *lāʻau lapaʻau*; Ilocano and Tagalog for care-home operators; Japanese for Kuakini's early records.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** some Hawaiian healing knowledge is passed only within families or from teacher to student; ask practitioners and Papa Ola Lōkahi's councils. Elder abuse and care-home neglect cases are sensitive; use published reports.
- **Held for review:** an interfaith volunteer program for homebound elders that grew out of a Japanese Buddhist temple was weighed and held as a category.
