## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli kapa, featherwork and lauhala makers against contemporary Native Hawaiian painters and designers: check how each speaks of faithful copying versus new work.
- Niʻihau families and their shell lei: approach only through the community's school and spokespeople, since patterns and sources of shells may be family knowledge.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: compare ranked teaching lines in classical dance, music and *sanshin* with garage inventors and potters.
- Chinese and Korean families: check where craft skill was passed down and where it was dropped for professions.
- Filipino residents, Ilocano and Visayan: look at *diskarte* in work talk, and at rondalla and weaving revival classes.
- Portuguese descendants: trace the Madeiran cabinetmakers credited with the ʻukulele and how families tell it.
- Puerto Rican descendants: look for *cuatro* players and makers; sources are thin.
- Samoan and Tongan *tufuga* (tattoo, house-building) in Lāʻie and Kalihi: check who may practice and who may not.
- Marshallese and Chuukese women's handicraft sold at fairs (Kona, Honolulu): little in print; start with church groups.
- Haole and military residents, paniolo saddle and leather makers of Waimea, and art villages (Hōlualoa, Makawao): compare the market for art with the market for craft.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for Hawaiian craft revival and music history; mixed for creative-economy numbers; thin for Micronesian and Puerto Rican makers.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for composers' own words in the nūpepa and liner notes; Samoan and Tongan for *tufuga* practice; Marshallese for handicraft terms.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** some chants, hula and design lineages are owned and taught only within their hālau or family; seek the kumu's leave. Disputes over cultural appropriation are contested; give each side as an account.
- **Held for review:** empty; the maker and craft pointers weighed here (lineage arts, inventors, food) required nothing set aside.
