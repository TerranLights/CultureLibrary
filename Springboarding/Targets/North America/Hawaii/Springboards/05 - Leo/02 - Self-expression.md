## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli: compare chant and *mele* composers with activists and with *māhū* voices; check how *kaona* (hidden meaning) shapes what is said openly.
- People of Niʻihau, whose daily speech is Hawaiian: check, through their own school, whether print or media sources exist at all.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: compare what each generation, Issei to Gosei, reports holding back, and look at *eisaa* and taiko stages.
- Chinese and Korean families: compare church testimony and choir singing with what families say at home.
- Filipino residents: check Ilocano and Visayan generations in dance crews, pageants and hip hop.
- Portuguese and Puerto Rican descendants (Kula, Hilo, Kauaʻi): check how families' memoirs describe teasing and speaking out at gatherings.
- Samoan, Tongan, Marshallese and Chuukese residents: check church youth performances and skits; Micronesian material is thin.
- Haole residents, military families and North Shore surfers: look at dress, tattoos and decals as signs of where one stands.
- Hāna, Molokaʻi and Kaʻū against Honolulu: test whether small places hold expression in or let it out.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** mixed; music, literature and protest are well covered, measured expressiveness is thin.
- **Languages needed:** Pidgin for fiction and radio; Hawaiian for chant and *kaona*; Samoan and Tongan for dance commentary.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the overthrow and sovereignty events are contested ground; give each account as an account. Gender identity and coming out are sensitive; draw on what people chose to publish. Some chants carry family meanings not open to outsiders.
- **Held for review:** empty; among the voice, dress and protest pointers no item called for setting aside.
