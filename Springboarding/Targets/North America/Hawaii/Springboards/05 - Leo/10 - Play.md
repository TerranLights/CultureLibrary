## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli: compare old games (string figures, *kōnane*, *ʻulu maika*) taught in Hawaiian-immersion schools with everyday play; check who teaches the chants.
- People of Niʻihau: ask through the community's own school, never directly, about children's games.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: check table games and children's games carried from the camps.
- Chinese and Korean families: check where mahjong and *yunnori* still come out at holidays, and whether elders and children play together.
- Filipino residents, Ilocano and Visayan: street games and party games across generations.
- Portuguese and Puerto Rican descendants: check card games and music play at family gatherings.
- Samoan and Tongan residents: check *kilikiti*, card games and church youth nights, and who organizes each.
- Marshallese and Chuukese residents: look at sport and youth nights through community groups.
- Haole residents, military families and North Shore surfers: compare surf, skate and organized-league play.
- Paniolo families of Waimea and Upcountry Maui: check rodeo games and horse play for children.
- Rural Kaʻū, Hāna and Molokaʻi against Honolulu: test where children may roam alone.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for traditional games and surf history; mixed for children's free play; thin for Micronesian youth.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for string-figure chants; Japanese and Korean for card-game terms; Samoan for *kilikiti* rules and calls.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** card play can involve betting; record it as play and gathering, not as charges (law: Libra, Capricorn). Some chants with string figures may be family-held; ask first.
- **Held for review:** empty; the game, toy and park pointers looked at here needed nothing held back.
