# Hawaii: Work (Virgo): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi; Pidgin and the English of Hawaii; Japanese; Cantonese; Korean; Ilocano and Tagalog; Portuguese; Samoan and Tongan; Marshallese and Chuukese through community groups. **Concept in one line:** purposeful effort that produces, keeps up or provides something of value, paid or unpaid, and the place that effort holds in a life.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kuaʻāina families of Keʻanae-Wailuanui, east Molokaʻi and Miloliʻi: check how gathering and fishing are weighed against wage jobs, and who in a household does which.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: compare Issei contract laborers' accounts with those of Nisei and Sansei professionals on what work is for.
- Ilocano and Visayan Filipino families (Waipahu, Kahuku, west Kauaʻi, Lānaʻi): separate the *sakada* generation from post-1965 arrivals in hotel and care jobs.
- Chinese and Korean descendants: trace the move from field to shop and profession and how families narrate it.
- Portuguese and Puerto Rican descendants: check how luna and skilled-trade posts were handed out and remembered.
- Samoan and Tongan workers in stevedoring, construction and security: check union rolls and church accounts.
- Marshallese and Chuukese workers on Kona coffee farms and in Honolulu hotels and warehouses: few studies exist; look for community groups' reports.
- Paniolo of Parker Ranch, ʻUlupalakua and Molokaʻi Ranch: compare the standing of ranch work with field work.
- Haole residents: separate planter and manager families from newcomers in medicine, tech and remote jobs.
- Military households and civilian shipyard workers: check how the federal pay scale and trades ladder differ from private work.
- Niʻihau families: shell-lei making as income; only through the community.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich on plantation and union history and on labor statistics; thin on informal work, Micronesian workers and Niʻihau.
- **Languages needed:** Japanese for work-song texts and camp-era labor columns; Ilocano for union leaflets and sakada letters; Hawaiian for Malo in the original.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the longliner crews and the treatment of Micronesian farm workers are live disputes; give each side as an account. Readings of the 1946–49 strikes and the union's left wing differ sharply; Horne's is one.
- **Held for review:** empty. Union papers, planter records, ranch history and cane-field songs were weighed here, and none falls under a held category.
