## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli in sovereignty groups and in state offices: compare what *mana* and power mean in each setting, through their own statements.
- Kānaka Maoli of Hawaiian Home Lands communities (Papakōlea, Keaukaha): check how homestead associations press their claims.
- People of Niʻihau: check, only through community members, how power on a privately held island is spoken of.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: compare the generation that entered office after 1954 with their grandchildren's view of that rise.
- Chinese and Korean descendants: check business and church networks as routes to influence.
- Filipino families (Ilocano, Visayan): check how a candidate draws on hometown associations.
- Portuguese and Puerto Rican families of Maui and Hilo: check union and county politics as routes to standing.
- Samoan and Tongan communities of Lāʻie and Kalihi: check church and title holders as power brokers.
- Marshallese and Chuukese residents: check how they reach officials without a state vote; sources are scant.
- Military families and haole newcomers: compare the power of the base with the power of old island families.
- Kauaʻi residents: compare seed-company workers' and opponents' accounts of the 2013 fight.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for elections, money, land and the military; mixed for informal influence; thin for Micronesian and Niʻihau voices.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for Kamakau and the nūpepa; Ilocano for hometown-association politics; Japanese for issei and nisei political papers.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** Kūkāʻilimoku images are sacred to many; follow museum and cultural protocol. The military's land, the 2013 Kauaʻi fight and sovereignty claims are contested; give each account. Authority as such is Capricorn's.
- **Held for review:** nothing was held for power; every candidate here (war god, party, money, land, church landlord, utility) passed the standing rules as it stood.
