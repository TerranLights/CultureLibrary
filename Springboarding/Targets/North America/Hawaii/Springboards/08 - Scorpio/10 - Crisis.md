## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli of Kaʻū and Puna: check how the 1868 disaster and later eruptions sit in family memory and in Pele tradition.
- Kānaka Maoli of the Waiʻanae Coast: compare Iwa's damage with how the coast cares for its own in later storms.
- People of Niʻihau: check, through community members, how the island's storms and shortages are met with Kauaʻi kin.
- Japanese and Okinawan families of Kona: compare 2011 with the family stories of 1946 and 1960.
- Ilocano and Visayan families: check how relief abroad competes with crises at home for the same household money.
- Samoan and Tongan families: check church-led relief and its strain on Hawaii households.
- Chuukese and Marshallese families: check where help is sought in a crisis; documentation is scarce, so ask community groups first.
- Korean and Chinese descendants: check how family firms weathered 2001 and 2020.
- Portuguese and Puerto Rican families of Hilo and Upcountry Maui: compare old plantation-era hardship talk with present crises.
- Military families and haole newcomers: compare their crisis networks (base, church, online) with long-settled families'.
- Lahaina residents: compare trust in relief funds by length of residence and ancestry.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for disasters and visitor statistics; mixed for household and mental-health crises; thin for Micronesian and Niʻihau experience.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for 1868 letters and famine accounts; Ilocano and Tagalog for relief networks; Japanese for Kona coverage of 2011.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the Lahaina fire is recent and painful; relief decisions are contested, so present each account. Crisis-team and SNAP data concern vulnerable people; use published totals only.
- **Held for review:** for crisis, nothing was withheld; the relief networks considered ran through churches, associations and public agencies that the standing rules allow.
