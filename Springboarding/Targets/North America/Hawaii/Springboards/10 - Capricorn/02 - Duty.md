# Hawaii: Duty (Capricorn): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi; Pidgin; Hawaii English; Japanese and Uchinaaguchi; Tagalog and Ilocano; Samoan; Tongan; Korean; Cantonese; Portuguese; Marshallese and Chuukese through community interpreters. **Concept in one line:** what a person owes and must do because of a role, a tie or a promise, whether or not they wish to.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli of Waipiʻo, Hanalei and Keʻanae taro valleys: check how duties to water, land and neighbors are assigned and enforced.
- Kānaka Maoli in the royal societies and civic clubs (Honolulu, Hilo, Kona): check how ceremonial duties to the aliʻi are inherited or volunteered.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants (Hilo, Waipahu, Kona coffee country): separate the duties of the eldest son's household from those of daughters and younger sons.
- Ilocano and Visayan households: weigh what is owed to kin in the islands against what is sent to home provinces.
- Korean and Chinese descendants: look at who now pays for and attends parents' rites, generation by generation.
- Portuguese families of Kula, Pāʻia and Hilo: check who agrees to serve a year as *mordomo*, and why some families stop.
- Samoan and Tongan households: compare what a family event asks of each earner with household budgets; studies are few.
- Marshallese and Chuukese families under the Compacts: check what kin duties survive distance and island poverty, through their own groups.
- Service families and recent haole arrivals: count jury service and volunteering among the short-stay and the newly settled.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** strong for kingdom labor rules and wartime service; middling for caregiving; weak for Micronesian and Tongan obligations.
- **Languages needed:** the labor-day complaints are in Hawaiian; mourning vocabulary needs Tagalog and Ilocano; repatriation collections need Marshallese and Chuukese speakers; Issei correspondence needs Japanese.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the public land trust is contested ground, with state, OHA and sovereignty accounts to set side by side; funerals and repatriations are family matters, approached through pastors and families.
- **Held for review:** one pointer, on the yearly rites Japanese households owe their dead, is held as a category (Buddhism).

## 5. First queries
1. Ulukau or Papakilo: "poalima" in the 1840 and 1846 statutes and in 1840s nūpepa letters.
2. Library catalog: Miyamoto "Hawaii, end of the rainbow"; Hawaii War Records Depository: 442nd "Iolani Palace" 1943.
3. OHA and Legislative Reference Bureau sites: "public land trust" revenue share; read the state's and OHA's figures side by side.
4. CDC BRFSS: identify the years Hawaii ran the caregiver module; tabulate caregivers by county.
5. Interview list: taro-farmer associations in Waipiʻo and Hanalei; Chuukese and Marshallese pastors in Kalihi and Kona.
