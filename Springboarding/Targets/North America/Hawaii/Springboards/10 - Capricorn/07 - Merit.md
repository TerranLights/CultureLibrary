## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli in hālau, canoe clubs and Hawaiian-medium schools: compare standing earned through a teacher's test with standing from genealogy.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants: check how the postwar move into teaching, law and civil service is told, by them and by others who felt shut out.
- Chinese descendants of Honolulu: compare merchant-family succession with professional careers chosen by examination.
- Filipino families (Ilocano, Visayan, Tagalog speakers): check whether *palakasan* talk is used about island workplaces or only about the Philippines.
- Korean Honolulu: set early students' scholarship stories beside later arrivals' fights to have Korean degrees recognized.
- Portuguese descendants (Kula, Hilo, Kauaʻi): see whether an inside-favor word (*empenho*, *cunha*) survives in talk about county jobs.
- Samoan and Tongan families: check whether athletic scholarships are spoken of as earned by the player or owed to the family.
- Chuukese and Marshallese residents: begin with hiring managers and community groups on whether home degrees are accepted.
- Haole and military families: compare transfers of rank and license from the continent with how locals see newcomers placed above them.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** solid for scholarships, tests and the civil service; uneven for patronage (reported case by case); scant for COFA credentials.
- **Languages needed:** the 1845 petitions and contest notices are Hawaiian texts; prewar graduate lists are in Japanese; *palakasan* talk lives in Tagalog radio and papers.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** training rites belong to hālau and practitioners; the 1845 petitions and postwar "who got the jobs" stories are contested, so give each side's account.
- **Held for review:** one long-running civic honors program, sponsored by a temple body, was set aside as a category (Buddhism).
