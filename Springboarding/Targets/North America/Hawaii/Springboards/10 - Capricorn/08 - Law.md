# Hawaii: Law (Capricorn): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi; Hawaii English and Pidgin; Japanese; Tagalog and Ilocano; Samoan and Tongan; Korean; Cantonese; Portuguese; Chuukese and Marshallese through legal-aid interpreters. **Concept in one line:** the binding rules of a community and how people relate to them: what law is for, whose it is, whether it is just, and whether to obey it, use it, bend it or avoid it. This sheet takes the spirit of the law; its letter is in Libra.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli in sovereignty groups and in state service: set kingdom-continuity claims beside the views of Kānaka Maoli judges and legislators, each in their own words.
- Kānaka Maoli of Puna, Kaʻū and Hāna: compare how cannabis growers, kūpuna and police describe enforcement there.
- Okinawan and Naichi families: follow the line from relatives on 1941 detention lists to grandchildren on the bench, in family memory.
- Ilocano and Visayan Kauaʻi and Waipahu: listen for how cockfight raids are talked about, as crime, custom or nuisance.
- Chinese and Korean Honolulu: note when society halls and church sessions stopped ending quarrels and members began to sue.
- Samoan and Tongan households: after a fight, ask who is called first (pastor, titled elder or 911) and why.
- Chuukese and Marshallese families: start with legal-aid lawyers and interpreters on how often they reach a court at all; records are thin.
- Service members off base: set their units' conduct rules beside county ordinances and ask which they fear breaking.
- Haole newcomers in Puna and on the North Shore: check off-grid settlers' and surfers' talk of laws they ignore, and why.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** strong for kingdom law-making and the overthrow's competing claims; moderate for compliance and court use; weak for informal settlement and Micronesian experience.
- **Languages needed:** sovereignty writing and the chiefs' lessons are partly Hawaiian; detention-era and early-bar papers partly Japanese; talk of rules on Filipino radio is Tagalog and Ilocano.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the overthrow, kingdom continuity, Mauna Kea and land struggles are contested; give each account as one. Organized-crime coverage names living families; use court records, not rumor.
- **Held for review:** none; the legal-culture pointers came from courts, firms, sovereignty writers and island press, and no community or scholar needed holding.
