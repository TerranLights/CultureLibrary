# Hawaii: Order (Capricorn): springboard

*Scouting, not research. From the agent's own knowledge; nothing here is verified. Every name is marked *check*.*
*Use: a starting point for research, not its limit. Know why each pointer is here, check it, then follow every relevant lead beyond it (RUNBOOK, "Research from the springboard").*

**Target:** Hawaii (the Hawaiian Islands), region North America. **Language(s) for searching:** Pidgin; ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi; Hawaii English; Japanese; Tagalog and Ilocano; Cantonese; Korean; Samoan and Tongan; Portuguese; Marshallese and Chuukese in housing and outreach records. **Concept in one line:** things and people being where and as they should be: the arrangement that holds off chaos, in the cosmos, society, the home and the self.

## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Kānaka Maoli of Waiʻanae and Waimānalo: compare *ahupuaʻa* and *kuleana* ideas of where things belong with county zoning maps.
- Kānaka Maoli of Lahaina and Hāna: ask how festival crowds and visitor traffic are seen as disorder, and by whom.
- Japanese and Okinawan descendants of Mōʻiliʻili and Hilo: look at how yard and neighborhood standards are kept, through associations or neighbors' talk.
- Waipahu and west Kauaʻi neighborhoods with many Ilocano and Visayan households: list which parties, roosters and parked cars draw complaints, and from whom.
- Chinese descendants of Chinatown: set Hotel Street's wartime order and later "clean and safe" drives beside elders' memories.
- Samoan and Tongan families in Kalihi housing: check how residents' own patrols and church youth nights compare with police presence.
- Chuukese and Marshallese households: check overcrowding rules against kin obligations to house relatives.
- Haole newcomers in Puna and Kona: compare off-grid settlers who choose disorder with newer arrivals who file noise complaints.
- Military families in Mililani and ʻEwa: check on- and off-base housing rules against what neighbors expect.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** planners, zoning boards and the wartime military left thick files; neighbor complaints exist mostly as raw counts; the Compact households' side of enforcement is largely unwritten, and Niʻihau can be reached only through its own people.
- **Languages needed:** Hawaiian for chiefly death rites and *haunaele* reports; Ilocano for union accounts of 1938; Japanese for prewar neighborhood association minutes.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** rites at a chief's death touch burials and family-held knowledge; encampment sweeps and the 1938 shooting are disputed, so give each account as one.
- **Held for review:** none; zoning, festival, wartime and complaint records carried this concept, and no source here fell under the rules on communities, scholars or religion.
