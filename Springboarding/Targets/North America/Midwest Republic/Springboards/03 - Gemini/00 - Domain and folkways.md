## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- **Inland North (Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, Milwaukee), North Central (Minnesota, the Upper Peninsula) and Midland (Columbus, Indianapolis, St. Louis, Kansas City):** check whether talk norms follow the atlases' lines or cut across them.
- **Chicagoland against central and southern Illinois:** check how neighbor and visiting surveys differ by size of place.
- **Black Midwesterners of Bronzeville and Detroit:** check how Great Migration families carried Southern speech ways north and reshaped them.
- **Amish and Mennonite settlements (Elkhart-LaGrange, Holmes County, Kalona; Adams County's Swiss Amish):** check which of Deitsch, English and High German serves which setting.
- **Anishinaabeg (Ojibwe, Odawa, Potawatomi), Dakota, Ho-Chunk, Menominee, Meskwaki, the Oneida of Wisconsin:** check each nation's own account of speaking and storytelling; never merge them.
- **Dearborn's Lebanese, Yemeni and Iraqi families; Detroit's Chaldeans; Hmong of St. Paul; Somali of Minneapolis and Columbus; Bosnians of St. Louis:** check generational language shift, newest arrivals against oldest.
- **Mexican Americans and Puerto Ricans (Pilsen, Little Village, Humboldt Park, southwest Detroit):** check Spanish-English switching and contact between the two.
- **Upper Peninsula and Iron Range towns; Appalachian out-migrants in Cincinnati, Dayton and Uptown:** check mixed and carried-in speech (Finglish, Yooper English, Southern Upland forms).

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for English dialects, Chicago and Detroit; mixed (mostly historical) for German, Scandinavian and Polish; thin for Ho-Chunk, Menominee, Meskwaki, Bosnian and Chaldean everyday talk, where community language programs and local histories are the route. The nation's exact lands are still to be settled; the sheets are written for the Great Lakes and upper Mississippi Valley core, with Appalachian Ohio, the Plains, the CSA and New England as comparison only.
- **Languages needed:** English, each studied community's home language, and Anishinaabemowin and Dakota for the oldest speech and place terms.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** some Anishinaabe sacred stories are told only in certain seasons, as communities state; ask before using recordings.
- **Held for review:** a country-level directness guide was dropped as too coarse for regional talk; nothing on this sheet needed holding under the standing rules.
