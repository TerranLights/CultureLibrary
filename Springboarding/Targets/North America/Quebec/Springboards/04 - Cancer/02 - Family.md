## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Charlevoix and Bas-Saint-Laurent rural families: check how kin reach in old parishes differs from new suburbs, in local histories.
- Saint-Hyacinthe and other Montérégie mill towns: compare 19th-century wage families with farm families nearby.
- Anglophone dynasties and working families of Montreal: separate elite archives from Pointe-Saint-Charles and Verdun oral history.
- Kanien'kehá:ka of Kahnawà:ke and Akwesasne: check how clan belonging passes and who speaks for it.
- Atikamekw of Manawan, Wemotaci and Opitciwan: check kin terms and grandparents' roles from community sources.
- Mi'gmaq of Listuguj and Gesgapegiag: compare what "all my relations" covers, in the nation's own teaching.
- Italian, Greek, Portuguese and Colombian Montrealers: compare sponsorship of kin and family enterprises, community by community.
- Chinese Montrealers: check surname associations as family substitutes in the head-tax era.
- Families formed by adoption and same-sex parents: compare how each names kin.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for family history and demography; mixed for immigrant family life (strong for some communities, thin for Latin American ones); Indigenous kinship to be taken from each nation.
- **Languages needed:** French for sociology and fiction; English for Anglophone archives; Kanien'kéha, Atikamekw and Mi'gmaq for kin and clan words; Italian, Greek, Portuguese and Spanish for life histories; Cantonese for association records.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** status rules on marriage, adoption files and youth-protection records are closed or painful; clan knowledge may be taught only in community. Family law (marriage, filiation) goes to Libra.
- **Held for review:** kinship-network, adoption and dynasty pointers were checked under the standing rules for this concept; none had to be held back.
