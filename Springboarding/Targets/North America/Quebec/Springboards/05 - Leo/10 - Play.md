## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Capitale-Nationale and the Saguenay: check winter play customs (slides, outdoor rinks) against Montreal's.
- Montreal suburbs and small towns of the Laurentides: compare street-play disputes and how each settled them.
- Bas-Saint-Laurent and Mauricie farm families: check the corn-husking party and older games in local memory.
- Inuit of Nunavik and Naskapi of Kawawachikamach: compare string figures and children's games as each community teaches them.
- Mi'gmaq of Listuguj and Gesgapegiag: check which games are taught in schools and at gatherings.
- Kanien'kehá:ka of Akwesasne and Kahnawà:ke: compare winter games and their revival.
- Italian, Greek and Portuguese Montrealers: check where men's park and café games persist across generations.
- Haitian Montrealers: compare riddle and tale sessions, dominoes and dance as play in family memory.
- Anglophone Montrealers and Townships families: check whether English-language schools and camps keep separate game repertoires.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for policy, leisure research and media; mixed for children's folklore; thin for Naskapi and Mi'gmaq games outside community sources.
- **Languages needed:** French for school, municipal and leisure research; English for Turner and Anglophone camps; Inuktitut, Naskapi and Mi'gmaq for games' own names; Italian and Greek for neighborhood games.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** some Indigenous games carry ceremonial meaning and are shared only as communities choose. Turner's report reflects its time. Gambling is in the domain sheet; competition goes to Aries.
- **Held for review:** for play, the games, toys and leisure-research pointers were reviewed and nothing was held back.
