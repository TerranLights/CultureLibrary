## 3. Who to compare inside the nation
- Montreal's francophone and Anglophone bars: check where civil-law and common-law habits meet in contracts.
- The Beauce and the Saguenay–Lac-Saint-Jean: compare handshake deals and notarized ones in local business histories.
- The Gaspé and the Magdalen Islands: check fishing partnerships and Acadian marriage customs in parish and notarial records.
- Eeyou of Eeyou Istchee and Inuit of Nunavik: compare how each tells the 1975 agreement and its later amendments.
- Kanien'kehá:ka of Kahnawà:ke, Kanehsatà:ke and Akwesasne: check the community's own justice bodies beside provincial courts.
- Innu, Atikamekw and Anishinaabeg: check which negotiation each is in, in its own words.
- Haitian Montrealers: compare customary union (*plase*), civil marriage and church weddings across generations.
- Hasidic and other Jewish Montrealers: check where the Beth Din and the civil courts divide matters.
- Maghrebi and Lebanese Montrealers: check how religious marriage and the code are joined or kept apart.

## 4. Flags
- **Data:** rich for codes, rulings and notarial archives; mixed for courtship and quarrel customs; thin for First Nations' legal orders beyond what each nation publishes. The nation's exact lands are still to be settled; this sign is written for Quebec as it stands, with Labrador, the Maritimes and Franco-Ontario as comparison only.
- **Languages needed:** legal French, old and new; English for federal law; Kanien'kéha, Eeyou and Inuktitut for treaty teachings; Haitian Creole and Yiddish for community marriage.
- **Sensitive or restricted:** the James Bay agreements, the 1990 Kanehsatà:ke resistance and the language and secularism laws carry rival accounts; present each as an account. Wampum teachings belong to their keepers. The spirit of the law goes to Capricorn.
- **Held for review:** among the court, treaty, tribunal and folk-tale pointers weighed for the domain, none needed setting aside under the standing rules.
